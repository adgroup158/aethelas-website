Verifiable Unlearning: New Research Results

September 1, 2026 6 min read Akash Deep

Regulations like India's DPDP Act and the EU's GDPR grant individuals the right to have their data erased. For AI systems, this is extraordinarily hard: proving that specific data was removed, and that its removal didn't disturb unrelated knowledge, is non‑trivial.

Today, we're publishing Technical Report 10 — our empirical results on provable machine unlearning. The findings are significant: we've demonstrated selective, verifiable deletion on a content‑addressed storage engine with cryptographic proof of erasure.

The Problem

Simply deleting a raw data record doesn't remove that record's influence from a model's already‑trained parameters. The machine unlearning literature studies how to remove this influence without full retraining from scratch — but we're not aware of a widely deployed method that also produces a verifiable proof of removal for a third‑party auditor.

This gap matters because regulators need more than a promise — they need evidence. A user invoking the right to be forgotten needs assurance not that data is stored, but that it is gone.

🔒 What we built: A content‑addressed storage engine that removes a contributor's data, recomputes a Merkle‑like root hash, and provides cryptographic evidence that the stored state changed.

The Experiment

We ingested data for two contributors:

  • Alice: 8 entries (5 notes, 3 chat messages)
  • Bob: 5 entries

We then unlearned Alice's data and measured the results:

  • Alice's entries: 8 → 0 (100% removed)
  • Bob's entries: 5 → 5 (bit‑for‑bit intact)
  • Root hash: be42e712d88fb38c06e47e55d7eab3d7 → 24638f6f7a056c5f68e72d481f1b0693

The combination — target fully removed, unrelated data preserved, root hash changed — is the empirical definition of selective, provable unlearning.

Why This Matters

The deletion proof (the post‑deletion root hash) can be anchored to a blockchain so that the fact and time of erasure are themselves permanently auditable. For regulated industries, this provides:

  • Regulatory compliance — DPDP Act and GDPR audits can verify erasure
  • Third‑party verification — Auditors don't need to trust the operator
  • Tamper‑evident records — The root hash change proves the stored state changed
  • Selectivity proof — Unrelated data remains bit‑for‑bit intact
"This demonstrates provable unlearning of stored memory — the retrievable knowledge a model reasons over — not the removal of information already absorbed into a neural network's weights."

Honest Scope

We're explicit about what this proves and what it doesn't:

  • What it proves: Memory‑level unlearning with cryptographic evidence
  • What it doesn't prove: Weight‑level unlearning for parametric models (a harder, separate problem)
  • Current scale: Two contributors, thirteen entries — scale testing is future work
  • Limitation: Soft‑deletion retains a hash record for audit — operators requiring physical erasure must purge the underlying store

What's Next

Our proposal in TR‑06 extends this mechanism toward verifiable unlearning of model weights via ledger rollback and replay. That's a much harder problem — one that we're transparently reporting as unresolved.
